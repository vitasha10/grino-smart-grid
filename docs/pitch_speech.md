**[S1 · Cover — 0:00]**
We are team twenty-seven, Grino: Mikayel, Vitaliy, Zakhar, Ara, and I am Radik. Our question: how to keep Armenian homes safe as rooftop solar grows?

**[S2 · Street at noon — 0:14]** *(pre-dawn; Radik raises his hand — the operator brings the sun up; the far ends of the lines turn red)*
Here is an Armenian village before sunrise. The sun comes up, the roofs push power into the same thin wires, and at the end of each street the voltage climbs past two hundred forty-two volts — the limit.

**[S3 · The numbers — 0:32]**
Industry experts estimate that one customer in three gets voltage above the limit; the regulator's own June data show at least a hundred and fifty thousand. And last year ENA received almost four thousand claims for appliances damaged by poor power quality.

**[S4 · Why not fixed — 0:51]**
China, Germany and Australia already require inverters to help control the voltage. Armenia does not, yet.

**[S5 · SunGuard — 0:58]** *(operator: SunGuard ON on lines A and B → panels keep working; line D still shuts down and restarts; +SunGuard on D; then "ENA adjusts the transformer" → every line within the limit)*
So we designed SunGuard, a small box next to the inverter. It watches the voltage every second, turns on the inverter's own voltage control and eases power down instead of shutting down — and shows it all on your phone over Wi-Fi. Look: with SunGuard the panels keep working; without it, the inverters shut down and restart. Then ENA adjusts the transformer where our map points, and every line is back within the limit — no new wires.

**[S8 · Under $50 — 1:30]**
Inside: a voltage sensor, a microcontroller with Wi-Fi, an inverter link and a power supply — about thirty dollars of parts.

**[S6 · Map — 1:38]**
In the regulator's data we found two thousand six hundred lines that go over the limit only at noon, most of them in Shirak and Yerevan.

**[S7 · Live demo — 1:47]** *(operator steps through: line → assumed solar → noon → table → conclusion)*
Here is a real one in Gyumri: with the cheapest fix it has no hours over the limit, and the same wires take one point seven times more solar.

**[S9 · Business — 1:58]**
Who buys it? Families at the end of these lines, whose panels switch off at noon; installers, who built nineteen thousand plants last year; and later ENA, for the map.

**[S6b · Economics — 2:10]**
It costs about forty-two dollars to make and sells for seventy-nine; five percent of today's solar plants is three thousand boxes in year one.

**[S10 · 24 hours — 2:21]**
In twenty-four hours we tested and dropped seven ideas, analysed eight hundred thousand rows of data and pivoted three times.

**[S10b · What works today — 2:31]**
The analysis, the model, the calculator and this demo work today; the box is designed, and everything is on GitHub.
